Unplug an SSD, leave it in a drawer, and the files can still be there years later. There is no battery keeping each bit alive. So what, physically, remembers the data?

Electrons, held inside structures designed to make escaping difficult.

What interests me is how that tiny physical detail reaches all the way up to software. It explains why an SSD can read data without destroying it, why changing a file usually means writing somewhere new, and why pressing Delete is different from erasing the flash underneath.

Let’s start with one cell, then follow a small file through the drive.

## Put electrons somewhere they cannot easily escape

A flash cell is a transistor with a charge-storage region. A voltage on its **control gate** influences whether current can flow through a semiconductor **channel**.

In a classic floating-gate cell, an electrically isolated conductor stores charge. Many modern 3D NAND designs instead use a **charge-trap layer**, commonly silicon nitride, with localized sites that hold electrons. These are different structures, but both preserve information through stored charge. [KIOXIA explains the distinction in its NAND endurance brief.](https://americas.kioxia.com/content/dam/kioxia/en-us/business/memory/mlc-nand/asset/KIOXIA_TBW_WAF_NAND_Endurance_Technical_Brief.pdf)

A simplified floating-gate cross-section looks like this:

```text
              Control gate
          ───────────────────
             Insulating layer
          ───────────────────
          Floating gate: e⁻ e⁻    ← stored charge
          ───────────────────
             Thin tunnel oxide
          ───────────────────
          Semiconductor channel
          source ──────── drain
```

This is a conceptual stack, not the geometry of every NAND chip. Vertical 3D cells wrap layers around a channel, and charge-trap cells replace the floating conductor with a trapping dielectric.

The immediate problem is obvious: if the storage region is insulated, how do electrons get into it?

## Quantum tunnelling makes programming possible

An insulating layer presents an energy barrier. In a classical picture, an electron without enough energy to cross that barrier would remain on its original side.

Quantum mechanics describes the electron with a wavefunction. Inside a finite barrier, that wavefunction decays rather than immediately becoming zero. If the barrier is sufficiently thin, there is a nonzero probability of finding the electron on the other side. This is **tunnelling**. It does not require drilling a hole through the insulator.

During programming, a strong electric field changes the barrier’s shape, making tunnelling much more likely. Classic NAND uses **Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling** to transfer electrons from the channel into the floating gate. [This device-level review describes the programming mechanism.](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-431X/6/2/16)

Think of two operating conditions:

| Condition | Electric field across the barrier | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Programming | Strong, deliberately applied | Appreciable electron transfer during a controlled pulse |
| Holding data | No programming field | Escape is sufficiently slow for nonvolatile storage |

The cell is not tunnelling at its programming rate all the time. That difference is how the same barrier can permit an intentional write and then retain charge for a long period.

## Stored charge changes an electrical threshold

The controller does not count individual electrons when reading a cell. It detects their electrical effect.

A transistor’s **threshold voltage** is the gate voltage needed to make its channel conduct. Adding negative charge to the storage region generally raises that threshold in the conventional NAND model.

For a simple single-bit cell, choose a read voltage between two threshold ranges:

- An erased cell has a lower threshold and conducts at that voltage.
- A programmed cell has a higher threshold and does not.

The conventional SLC interpretation calls the erased state `1` and the programmed state `0`. That is an encoding convention, not a universal rule that an electron “is a zero.” [KIOXIA illustrates this readout process.](https://www.kioxia.com/en-jp/rd/technology/nand-flash.html)

A read senses current through the channel. It does not empty the charge-storage region to discover what was there.

## More bits mean more threshold ranges

A cell can represent more than two states by placing its threshold into one of several ranges.

| NAND type | Bits per cell | Distinguishable states |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SLC | 1 | 2 |
| MLC, in the usual two-bit sense | 2 | 4 |
| TLC | 3 | 8 |
| QLC | 4 | 16 |

Three bits have eight possible combinations, so TLC needs eight threshold distributions. It does not contain three independent little storage compartments. The reader distinguishes ranges using reference voltages, then decodes the stored pattern. The exact pattern assigned to each range depends on the design. [KIOXIA’s multi-level explanation shows the relationship.](https://www.kioxia.com/en-jp/rd/technology/multi-level-cell.html)

More states increase capacity per cell. They also leave less room for drift and noise between neighboring ranges.

## Why can the data survive for years?

Once programming stops, the storage structure still contains charge. No continuous power supply is needed to preserve that physical condition. The surrounding barriers make charge movement slow enough that a readable threshold can persist for a long time.

But retention does not require every original electron to remain forever. It requires the cell to stay distinguishable as its intended state, with errors within what the drive can correct.

Charge can leak or redistribute, shifting threshold distributions. Experimental work on MLC NAND measures how retention age changes the useful read reference voltages and how adapting those references can recover data. [Cai and colleagues describe those results here.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.02819)

Retention depends on the NAND technology, its wear, and storage conditions. Higher temperatures generally accelerate relevant leakage mechanisms; a heavily cycled drive also has different margins from a fresh one. Dell’s [power-off retention guidance](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-us/000198930/ssd-data-retention-considerations-when-powering-off-systems-for-a-prolonged-duration) explains why a drive’s specification must be read with its conditions.

So “flash can retain data for years” describes a capability, not a blanket guarantee for any SSD in any drawer. A powered controller may perform maintenance, but an unplugged SSD cannot refresh itself.

There is a second lifetime limit: **endurance**. Repeated program/erase cycles stress the materials, eventually making reliable storage harder. Controllers use error correction and wear leveling to manage that limitation. Retention is how long stored information remains readable; endurance is how much rewriting the medium can withstand. [KIOXIA connects these wear mechanisms to controller protections.](https://americas.kioxia.com/content/dam/kioxia/en-us/business/memory/mlc-nand/asset/KIOXIA_TBW_WAF_NAND_Endurance_Technical_Brief.pdf)

## From one cell to an SSD

NAND connects cells in series into strings. Their arrangement lets the chip select cells electrically while operating on many bits together. The drive then manages larger units:

- A **page** is a unit used for programming and reading NAND data.
- An **erase block** contains many pages that must be erased together.
- The **controller** translates host requests into flash operations.

Page sizes, block sizes, programming sequences, and multi-bit organization vary by generation. A host’s logical sector is not necessarily one physical NAND page.

The key restriction is that NAND cannot accept arbitrary new contents over an already programmed page. For general reuse, it needs a block erase. Samsung’s [NAND and flash-management overview](https://download.semiconductor.samsung.com/resources/white-paper/Samsung_SSD_White_Paper.pdf) explains this mismatch between small writes and larger erases.

That restriction is why an SSD needs a **flash translation layer**, or FTL. The computer addresses logical blocks; the FTL tracks where their current contents live physically.

## Example: write a small file

Suppose I save `notes.txt`, containing `Hello NAND`.

To keep the bookkeeping visible, imagine its data fits in one logical unit called **L42**, and the controller places it into physical page **A0**. These are invented labels. Real files also involve filesystem metadata, and real SSDs combine, split, encode, and distribute writes across many cells.

First, the filesystem allocates storage and issues writes. The SSD usually receives block addresses and bytes, not the filename `notes.txt`.

The controller chooses available erased space, prepares the data with error-correction information, and programs the target cells. Programming uses pulses and verification to reach the intended states. It also preserves enough mapping information to find the data again.

Our simplified map now says:

```text
Logical address       Physical location
L42                   Block A, page A0

Block A:  [A0: Hello NAND] [A1: other data] [A2: erased]
```

The file becomes persistent only once the relevant data and metadata have reached durable storage under the system’s write/flush guarantees. A save can initially land in an operating-system or device cache; the application returning from a write is not, by itself, proof that every bit has reached NAND.

## Read the file back

Assume this read misses the operating system’s cache and reaches the SSD.

The controller resolves **L42 → A0** and asks the flash to read that page. The chip applies read and pass voltages so that the selected cells’ thresholds determine the sensed result. Data then passes through decoding and error correction before the controller returns bytes to the host.

If the first attempt has too many errors, the drive may retry with adjusted read references. Error correction has limits; it is not a promise that arbitrarily damaged cells can be recovered. KIOXIA’s [reliability handbook](https://www.kioxia.com/content/dam/kioxia/en-jp/about/asset/reliability-handbook-e.pdf) covers retention and read-disturb failure mechanisms.

The electrons remain stored during ordinary reads. Repeated reading can nevertheless disturb cells over time, which the controller must manage.

## Edit the file: write somewhere new

Now change the contents to `Hello SSD!`.

Instead of erasing Block A just to replace A0, the controller writes the new version into another available page, say **B0**, then updates the mapping. A0 becomes stale.

```text
Before:  L42 → A0 → Hello NAND
After:   L42 → B0 → Hello SSD!

Block A: [A0: stale]      [A1: other live data] [A2: erased]
Block B: [B0: Hello SSD!] [B1: erased]          [B2: erased]
```

The logical address stays the same while its physical location changes. Old charge patterns can remain in A0 even though ordinary reads now resolve to B0. This is the core indirection described in the [SPDK SSD internals guide](https://spdk.io/doc/ssd_internals.html).

## Delete the file: three different events

When I delete `notes.txt`, there are several layers involved. Moving it to a trash folder may not even free its storage yet. Assume it is actually removed and its storage released.

**First, the filesystem stops treating the file’s blocks as allocated.** That changes filesystem metadata. It does not automatically send an erase pulse to every cell that held the file.

**Next, the operating system can tell the SSD that those logical addresses are no longer needed.** SATA calls this TRIM; NVMe provides deallocation through Dataset Management. Notification can be delayed or batched, and depends on the storage stack passing it through.

The SSD can now treat the corresponding contents as unnecessary during reclamation. Samsung describes [TRIM as a way to avoid copying invalid data during garbage collection](https://semiconductor.samsung.com/consumer-storage/support/faqs/internalssd-product-information/). It is not a promise of immediate physical erasure or secure sanitization. Reads after deallocation also depend on device behavior; they are not a reliable view into old charge patterns.

**Finally, garbage collection reclaims an erase block.** In our example, Block A still contains A1’s live data. The controller copies that elsewhere, updates its mapping, and erases all of Block A. A0’s old version is reclaimed along with the rest. B0’s deleted version can be reclaimed when its own block is selected.

Physically, erase changes the charge state back toward the erased threshold range across the block. In the floating-gate picture, an applied field drives electrons out through the tunnel barrier. Charge-trap erase mechanisms vary, so this should not be read as a universal voltage recipe for every 3D cell.

## Why these details matter to software

An SSD presents something that looks like freely rewritable blocks. Underneath, it programs new locations, moves valid data, and erases larger regions.

Those internal copies create **write amplification**: NAND can receive more written bytes than the host sent. Wear leveling distributes erase cycles rather than repeatedly exhausting the same physical blocks. Spare capacity gives the controller room to relocate data. Samsung’s [data-placement discussion](https://semiconductor.samsung.com/news-events/tech-blog/flexible-data-placement/) connects page placement, garbage collection, and internal write overhead.

That is the connection I find most satisfying: the way electrons cross an insulating barrier helps explain why editing ten bytes can eventually cause much more data to move inside a drive.

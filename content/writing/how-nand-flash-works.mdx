An SSD keeps your files without power by trapping charge inside its memory cells. The controller reads the effect of that charge to recover the bits.

Writing, updating, and deleting data all depend on how the cells hold and release it.

## Trap electrons inside a cell

A flash cell is a transistor with a charge-storage region. Its **control gate** controls current through a semiconductor **channel**. Stored electrons change how much gate voltage it takes to make that channel conduct.

Classic NAND stores charge in an insulated conductor called a **floating gate**. Many modern 3D NAND designs use localized traps in an insulating layer instead. Both structures retain charge without continuous power. [KIOXIA explains both designs.](https://americas.kioxia.com/content/dam/kioxia/en-us/business/memory/mlc-nand/asset/KIOXIA_TBW_WAF_NAND_Endurance_Technical_Brief.pdf)

### How do electrons cross an insulator?

The insulator presents an energy barrier. Quantum mechanics gives an electron a nonzero probability of crossing a sufficiently thin barrier, even without enough energy to pass over it. This is quantum tunnelling. The insulator remains intact.

During programming, a strong electric field reshapes the barrier and makes tunnelling much more likely. Classic NAND uses **Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling** to move electrons from the channel into the floating gate. Programming uses controlled pulses and verification to reach the intended state. [The device physics is described here.](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-431X/6/2/16)

With the programming field removed, escape becomes very slow. The cell can retain its state without power and still be rewritten with an applied field.

## Read the charge’s effect

The controller does not count electrons. It measures their effect on **threshold voltage**: the gate voltage needed to turn the transistor on.

In the conventional single-bit model, stored negative charge raises the threshold. Apply a read voltage between the erased and programmed ranges: the erased cell conducts; the programmed cell does not. Conventionally, those states mean `1` and `0`. [KIOXIA illustrates this readout.](https://www.kioxia.com/en-jp/rd/technology/nand-flash.html)

More bits per cell means distinguishing more threshold ranges:

| Type | Bits per cell | Threshold states |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SLC | 1 | 2 |
| MLC, usually | 2 | 4 |
| TLC | 3 | 8 |
| QLC | 4 | 16 |

TLC stores one of eight distinguishable states, not three independent charges in separate compartments. More states improve density but tighten the margins for noise and drift. [More on multi-level storage.](https://www.kioxia.com/en-jp/rd/technology/multi-level-cell.html)

## Keep the data without power

The insulating barriers make charge movement slow enough to preserve readable states for long periods. Every electron need not stay forever; the cell must remain distinguishable as the intended state, within error-correction limits.

Charge can leak or redistribute over time. **Retention depends on temperature, wear, and the NAND design**—“can last for years” is not a guarantee for every SSD under every condition. A powered controller may perform maintenance; an unplugged drive cannot refresh itself. [Retention experiments](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.02819) and [Dell’s power-off guidance](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-us/000198930/ssd-data-retention-considerations-when-powering-off-systems-for-a-prolonged-duration) explain these limits.

Two different lifetimes matter:

- **Retention:** how long stored data remains readable.
- **Endurance:** how much programming and erasing the cells withstand.

Program/erase cycles gradually damage the materials. Error correction helps recover imperfect reads; wear leveling spreads rewriting across blocks. Neither makes the flash unlimited. [KIOXIA’s endurance brief](https://americas.kioxia.com/content/dam/kioxia/en-us/business/memory/mlc-nand/asset/KIOXIA_TBW_WAF_NAND_Endurance_Technical_Brief.pdf) connects these mechanisms.

## Follow a file through the SSD

NAND reads and programs pages, but erases whole blocks.

A block contains many pages. Arbitrarily replacing a programmed page’s contents requires erasing its block first. The controller avoids doing that for every update by writing elsewhere and changing a mapping. Its **flash translation layer (FTL)** maps logical addresses to physical flash locations. [Samsung’s overview](https://download.semiconductor.samsung.com/resources/white-paper/Samsung_SSD_White_Paper.pdf) and [SPDK’s internals guide](https://spdk.io/doc/ssd_internals.html) explain this organization.

Let’s save `notes.txt` containing `Hello NAND`. For this toy example, its data occupies logical address **L42**, initially mapped to physical page **A0**. Real files also have filesystem metadata, and logical units do not necessarily match physical pages.

### Write: turn bytes into charge states

The filesystem allocates storage and sends bytes and addresses—not usually the filename—to the SSD. The controller picks erased space, encodes data with error-correction information, programs cells, and preserves the mapping **L42 → A0**.

A write can first land in a cache. Persistence depends on the system’s write/flush guarantees and the device’s power-loss behavior; a write call returning alone does not prove every bit has reached NAND.

### Read: map, sense, correct

Assuming the request reaches the SSD rather than being served from a host cache, the controller resolves **L42 → A0**. The chip selects the cells, applies read and pass voltages, and senses current. Decoding and error correction recover the bytes.

A difficult read may trigger retries with adjusted references. Ordinary reads retain the charge, although repeated reads can disturb cells over time. [KIOXIA’s reliability handbook](https://www.kioxia.com/content/dam/kioxia/en-jp/about/asset/reliability-handbook-e.pdf) covers these failure mechanisms.

### Update: write a new page

Change the text to `Hello SSD!`. The controller programs **B0**, changes the map to **L42 → B0**, and marks A0 stale. Its old charge pattern can remain, but normal reads now find B0.

This indirection is what makes the host’s freely rewritable address space work on flash that needs block erases.

### Delete: release, notify, reclaim

Assume the file is actually removed, not merely moved to Trash.

1. **Release:** the filesystem marks its storage free.
2. **Notify:** the OS can tell the SSD those logical addresses are unnecessary—TRIM on SATA, deallocation on NVMe. This may be batched or delayed.
3. **Reclaim:** garbage collection later copies any other live pages out of a chosen block, updates their mappings, and erases that entire block.

A0 and B0 are reclaimed when their respective blocks are selected. The drive need not erase either one the instant the file disappears. [Samsung explains how TRIM lets garbage collection skip invalid data.](https://semiconductor.samsung.com/consumer-storage/support/faqs/internalssd-product-information/)

Physical erase returns cells toward their erased threshold range. In a floating-gate cell, an applied field moves electrons out through the tunnel barrier; charge-trap erase details vary by design.

**Deleting, deallocating, and physically erasing are separate events.** TRIM is not a secure-sanitization guarantee, and reading a deallocated address does not reliably expose old cell contents.

## The software consequence

Copying live pages during reclamation creates write amplification: more bytes written to NAND than the host supplied. Spare capacity gives the controller room to work. [Samsung’s data-placement explanation](https://semiconductor.samsung.com/news-events/tech-blog/flexible-data-placement/) connects those costs to page placement.

Even a small edit can therefore cause writes beyond the data that changed.
